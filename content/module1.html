Infusion Therapy & Drug Calculations for Paramedics

5.1 Introduction to Infusion Therapy in Prehospital & Critical Care

Intravenous infusion therapy is a critical intervention in paramedic practice, enabling precise delivery of medications, fluids, blood products, and electrolytes directly into the circulatory system. Mastery of infusion calculations is essential for patient safety — calculation errors can result in under-dosing (therapeutic failure), overdosing (toxicity, cardiac arrest, respiratory depression), or incorrect fluid administration rates (fluid overload, pulmonary oedema).

This module covers the theoretical foundation, practical calculation methods, common infusion types, medication-specific dosing, and safety protocols that underpin safe infusion practice in South African paramedic care.

5.2 Clinical Approach to Infusion Therapy: CCT & CDST Framework

5.2.1 Critical Care Transfer (CCT) Approach

When managing infusion therapy in the prehospital and inter-facility transfer context, apply the CCT (Critical Care Transfer) framework:

  • C - Continuous Monitoring: Vital signs, ECG, SpO₂, ETCO₂, and level of consciousness must be monitored continuously during infusion. Document baseline observations before, during, and after infusion.
  • C - Catheter & Line Assessment: Verify IV cannula patency, site integrity, and infusion device function. Check for signs of infiltration, phlebitis, or extravasation before and during transport.
  • T - Transport Preparation: Ensure adequate supply of infusion fluid, drugs, and backup equipment. Calculate total infusion duration and have sufficient supplies for the journey plus 30 minutes contingency.

5.2.2 Clinical Decision Support Tool (CDST) for Infusions

Use the CDST framework for every infusion decision:

StepQuestionAction
1. IndicationWhat is the clinical indication for this infusion?Verify against protocols/guidelines
2. DrugIs this the correct drug, dose, and route?Double-check calculation, concentration
3. PatientDoes the patient have allergies or comorbidities?Adjust for weight, renal/hepatic impairment
4. EquipmentIs the correct administration set and device available?Confirm pump compatibility, drip set type
5. MonitoringWhat observations are required and how often?Set parameters, document response
6. ContingencyWhat are the risks and what is the backup plan?Have reversal agents, alternative access ready

5.2.3 Infusion Lab Competencies

The following competencies are essential for paramedic infusion practice and should be practiced in a skills lab environment:

  1. IV Cannulation: Aseptic technique, vein selection, cannula sizing (14-24G), securing and documentation.
  2. Infusion Pump Operation: Programming volumetric pumps, loading syringe drivers, alarm recognition and troubleshooting.
  3. Gravity Drip Calculations: Manual calculation of drip rates using drop factor (10, 15, 20, 60 gtt/mL).
  4. Drug Reconstitution: Correct dilution of powdered medications to achieve target concentration.
  5. Weight-Based Dosing: Calculating mcg/kg/min, mg/kg doses for emergency drugs.
  6. Paediatric Infusions: Using Broselow tape, calculating microdrip rates, syringe driver setup.
  7. Blood Product Administration: Type and crossmatch verification, prime set, monitor for reactions.
  8. Infusion Troubleshooting: Infiltration, occlusion, air embolism, pump malfunction responses.

5.2 Types of Intravenous Infusions

Understanding the type of infusion prescribed determines the calculation method, equipment required (gravity drip vs. infusion pump), and monitoring frequency.

5.2.1 Continuous IV Infusion

Medications or fluids administered at a constant, uninterrupted rate over an extended period (hours to days). Common examples include maintenance fluids (crystalloids), vasopressors (noradrenaline, dopamine), sedatives (propofol, midazolam), and analgesics (morphine, fentanyl).

  • Flow rate units: mL/hour (volumetric pumps) or drops/minute (gravity drip sets).
  • Clinical indication: Maintain stable plasma concentration of drug; replace ongoing fluid losses; provide haemodynamic support.

5.2.2 Intermittent IV Infusion (IV Piggyback)

Medications given at discrete intervals, typically over 20–60 minutes, via a secondary IV line connected to the primary IV. Common examples: antibiotics (ceftriaxone, metronidazole), antiemetics (ondansetron), and some analgesics.

  • Flow rate units: mL/hour over the prescribed infusion duration.
  • Clinical indication: Peak-trough dosing strategy; drugs with short half-lives requiring repeated dosing.

5.2.3 Bolus IV Push (Direct IV)

Rapid injection of a medication directly into the venous circulation over seconds to 2–3 minutes. Examples: adrenaline (cardiac arrest), atropine, naloxone, diazepam.

  • Flow rate units: mg or mL per minute; often given undiluted or diluted in a small volume (5–10 mL).
  • Clinical indication: Emergency resuscitation; rapid onset required; no time for infusion setup.

5.2.4 Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN)

Nutritional support for patients unable to meet nutritional needs enterally, consisting of amino acids (protein), dextrose (carbohydrate), lipid emulsion (fat), electrolytes, vitamins, and trace elements, administered via central venous access.

  • Flow rate units: mL/hour via volumetric pump; TPN is hyperosmolar and requires central line access (subclavian, internal jugular, PICC line).
  • Clinical indication: Prolonged gut failure, bowel obstruction, severe malabsorption, major burns.

5.2.5 Subcutaneous Infusion

Slow delivery of medications or fluids into the subcutaneous tissue, commonly used for chronic pain management (morphine via syringe driver), anticoagulation (low molecular weight heparin), or palliative care hydration (hypodermoclysis).

  • Flow rate units: mL/hour (typical 0.5–2 mL/hour for analgesics).
  • Clinical indication: Poor IV access; home-based or palliative care; stable, predictable absorption required.

5.2.6 Blood & Blood Product Transfusion

Administration of packed red blood cells (PRBC), platelets, fresh frozen plasma (FFP), or cryoprecipitate. Requires strict cross-match, identification checks, and specific transfusion set (170-micron filter).

  • Flow rate units: mL/hour; PRBC typically given over 2–4 hours per unit unless rapid transfusion protocol for massive haemorrhage.
  • Clinical indication: Acute blood loss, anaemia, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia.

5.3 Drug Concentration & Dilution: Core Principles

5.3.1 Concentration Terminology

Drug concentration expresses the mass (or units) of drug per unit volume of solution.

UnitMeaningExample
mg/mLMilligrams per millilitreMorphine 10 mg/mL means 10 mg of morphine in every 1 mL of solution.
mcg/mLMicrograms per millilitre (1 mcg = 0.001 mg)Adrenaline 100 mcg/mL (same as 0.1 mg/mL).
% w/vPercentage weight/volume: grams of drug per 100 mL0.9% saline = 0.9 g NaCl per 100 mL = 9 g/L.
Units/mLBiological activity units (insulin, heparin)Heparin 1000 units/mL.
Ratio strength1:X = 1 gram in X mLAdrenaline 1:1000 = 1 g in 1000 mL = 1 mg/mL.

5.3.2 The Fundamental Dosage Calculation Formula

To calculate the volume required to deliver a desired dose:

Volume (mL) = Desired Dose (mg) ÷ Concentration (mg/mL)

Example 1: To deliver 8 mg of morphine from a 10 mg/mL ampoule:

Volume = 8 mg ÷ 10 mg/mL = 0.8 mL

Example 2: To deliver 500 mcg (0.5 mg) of adrenaline from a 1:1000 (1 mg/mL) ampoule:

Volume = 0.5 mg ÷ 1 mg/mL = 0.5 mL

5.3.3 Converting Between Ratio Strength, %, and mg/mL

Ratio Strength% Equivalentmg/mL
1:10000.1%1 mg/mL
1:10,0000.01%0.1 mg/mL (100 mcg/mL)
1:1001%10 mg/mL

Memory aid: Adrenaline 1:1000 (for IM anaphylaxis) = 1 mg/mL; Adrenaline 1:10,000 (for IV cardiac arrest) = 0.1 mg/mL.

5.4 Infusion Rate Calculations

5.4.1 Calculating mL/hour for Volumetric Infusion Pumps

Modern infusion pumps deliver a set volume per hour. To calculate the infusion rate in mL/hour when a dose (mg/hour or mcg/min) is prescribed:

Infusion Rate (mL/hour) = [Desired Dose × Volume of Solution] ÷ [Drug Amount in Solution]

Example 3: A patient requires dopamine 5 mcg/kg/min. Patient weight 70 kg. You have 200 mg dopamine in 250 mL of 5% dextrose.

  1. Calculate total dose required: 5 mcg/kg/min × 70 kg = 350 mcg/min = 21,000 mcg/hour = 21 mg/hour.
  2. Concentration of solution: 200 mg in 250 mL = 0.8 mg/mL.
  3. Infusion rate: 21 mg/hour ÷ 0.8 mg/mL = 26.25 mL/hour.

5.4.2 Calculating Drops per Minute (gtt/min) for Gravity Drip Sets

When an infusion pump is unavailable, gravity drip sets are used. The drop factor (calibrated on the giving set packaging) varies by manufacturer:

  • Macrodrip sets: 10, 15, or 20 drops/mL.
  • Microdrip (paediatric) sets: 60 drops/mL.

Formula:

Drop Rate (gtt/min) = [Volume (mL) × Drop Factor (gtt/mL)] ÷ Time (minutes)

Example 4: Infuse 1000 mL normal saline over 8 hours using a 20 gtt/mL macrodrip set.

  1. Time in minutes: 8 hours × 60 = 480 minutes.
  2. Drop rate: (1000 mL × 20 gtt/mL) ÷ 480 min = 20,000 ÷ 480 = 41.67 gtt/min (round to 42 gtt/min).

5.4.3 Weight-Based Infusions (mcg/kg/min)

Many critical care drugs are dosed by body weight and time (mcg/kg/min). A systematic approach prevents errors:

  1. Convert patient weight to kg (if given in lbs: divide by 2.2).
  2. Calculate total dose per minute: mcg/kg/min × patient weight (kg).
  3. Convert to mcg/hour (multiply by 60) or mg/hour (divide by 1000).
  4. Determine concentration (mg/mL or mcg/mL).
  5. Calculate mL/hour using the infusion rate formula above.

Example 5: Noradrenaline 0.1 mcg/kg/min for a 65 kg patient. You have 4 mg noradrenaline in 250 mL of 5% dextrose.

  1. Dose per minute: 0.1 mcg/kg/min × 65 kg = 6.5 mcg/min = 390 mcg/hour = 0.39 mg/hour.
  2. Concentration: 4 mg in 250 mL = 0.016 mg/mL.
  3. Infusion rate: 0.39 mg/hour ÷ 0.016 mg/mL = 24.375 mL/hour (round to 24.4 mL/hour).

5.5 Common Infusion Drugs in Paramedic Practice: Dosing & Calculations

5.5.1 Morphine Sulfate (Opioid Analgesic)

Indication: Moderate to severe pain, acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary oedema.
Standard concentration: 10 mg/mL (1 mL ampoule).
Typical adult dose: 2.5–10 mg IV bolus (titrated to effect), repeated every 5–10 minutes; maintenance infusion 1–5 mg/hour.

Calculation example: Administer 5 mg IV from a 10 mg/mL ampoule.

Volume = 5 mg ÷ 10 mg/mL = 0.5 mL

Adverse effects: Respiratory depression, hypotension, bradycardia, nausea. Reversal agent: Naloxone 0.4 mg IV.

5.5.2 Fentanyl (Synthetic Opioid)

Indication: Severe pain (trauma, burns), procedural sedation, rapid sequence intubation adjunct.
Standard concentration: 50 mcg/mL (2 mL or 10 mL ampoule).
Typical adult dose: 25–100 mcg IV bolus (0.5–2 mcg/kg); infusion 25–100 mcg/hour.

Calculation example: Administer 75 mcg IV from a 50 mcg/mL ampoule.

Volume = 75 mcg ÷ 50 mcg/mL = 1.5 mL

Note: Fentanyl is 80–100× more potent than morphine. Onset 1–2 minutes; duration 30–60 minutes. Risk: Chest wall rigidity with rapid high-dose administration.

5.5.3 Adrenaline (Epinephrine)

Indication: Cardiac arrest (VF/pVT, asystole, PEA), anaphylaxis, severe bradycardia, hypotension.
Concentrations:

  • 1:1000 (1 mg/mL): For IM administration (anaphylaxis: 0.5 mg IM = 0.5 mL).
  • 1:10,000 (0.1 mg/mL): For IV administration (cardiac arrest: 1 mg IV = 10 mL).

Cardiac arrest dose: 1 mg IV every 3–5 minutes during CPR.
Anaphylaxis dose: 0.5 mg IM (anterolateral thigh), repeat after 5 minutes if no response.

Calculation example: Prepare 1 mg adrenaline for IV cardiac arrest from a 1:1000 (1 mg/mL) ampoule by diluting to 1:10,000.

Draw up 1 mL (1 mg) of 1:1000 adrenaline, add 9 mL normal saline → 10 mL total volume (1 mg in 10 mL = 0.1 mg/mL = 1:10,000). Administer the entire 10 mL IV.

5.5.4 Amiodarone (Antiarrhythmic)

Indication: Refractory VF/pVT (after 3 DC shocks), stable VT, AF with rapid ventricular rate.
Standard concentration: 50 mg/mL (3 mL ampoule = 150 mg).
Cardiac arrest dose: 300 mg IV bolus (after 3rd shock), then 150 mg IV if VF/pVT persists.
Stable VT/AF dose: 150 mg IV over 10 minutes, then infusion 1 mg/min for 6 hours, then 0.5 mg/min.

Calculation example: Administer 300 mg amiodarone IV. You have 3 mL ampoules (150 mg each).

300 mg ÷ 150 mg/ampoule = 2 ampoules = 6 mL total

Caution: Causes hypotension (administer over 10 minutes if patient has output); phlebitis (dilute in 5% dextrose, not saline).

5.5.5 Normal Saline (0.9% NaCl)

Indication: Volume resuscitation (hypovolaemia, haemorrhage), maintenance fluid, drug dilution vehicle.
Composition: 0.9 g NaCl per 100 mL = 9 g/L = 154 mmol/L sodium, 154 mmol/L chloride (isotonic, osmolality 308 mOsm/L).
Typical adult dose: 500–1000 mL bolus for fluid resuscitation; 80–125 mL/hour maintenance.

Calculation example: Administer a 20 mL/kg fluid bolus for a shocked patient weighing 75 kg.

Volume = 20 mL/kg × 75 kg = 1500 mL (administer rapidly over 10–15 minutes via pressure bag or manual bolus)

Caution: Large volumes (>2 L) can cause hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis.

5.5.6 Lactated Ringer's Solution (Hartmann's Solution)

Indication: Volume resuscitation, trauma, surgery (more physiological than normal saline).
Composition: Sodium 131 mmol/L, potassium 5 mmol/L, calcium 2 mmol/L, chloride 111 mmol/L, lactate 29 mmol/L (metabolised to bicarbonate).
Advantages: Lower chloride, contains buffer, closer to plasma electrolyte composition.

Contraindication: Avoid in hyperkalaemia, severe liver failure (lactate metabolism impaired).

5.5.7 Ketamine (Dissociative Anaesthetic/Analgesic)

Indication: Severe pain (trauma), procedural sedation (fracture reduction, cardioversion), rapid sequence intubation (RSI) in haemodynamically unstable patients.
Standard concentration: 50 mg/mL (10 mL vial = 500 mg).
Analgesic dose: 0.2–0.5 mg/kg IV (e.g., 20–40 mg for 80 kg patient).
Sedation dose: 1–2 mg/kg IV for procedural sedation; 4–5 mg/kg IM if no IV access.

Calculation example: Administer 30 mg IV for analgesia from a 50 mg/mL vial.

Volume = 30 mg ÷ 50 mg/mL = 0.6 mL

Adverse effects: Emergence phenomena (hallucinations, agitation — reduced by benzodiazepine pre-medication), hypersalivation (give atropine or glycopyrrolate), transient hypertension/tachycardia. Contraindication: Severe hypertension, raised intracranial pressure (relative).

5.5.8 Midazolam (Benzodiazepine Sedative)

Indication: Seizures (status epilepticus), procedural sedation, acute agitation, RSI adjunct.
Standard concentration: 5 mg/mL (1 mL or 2 mL ampoule).
Seizure dose: 10 mg IM/IN (intranasal), or 5–10 mg IV (titrated 1–2 mg at a time).
Sedation dose: 0.05–0.1 mg/kg IV (2.5–5 mg for average adult).

Calculation example: Administer 7.5 mg IV for status epilepticus from a 5 mg/mL ampoule.

Volume = 7.5 mg ÷ 5 mg/mL = 1.5 mL

Adverse effects: Respiratory depression (especially with opioids), hypotension. Reversal agent: Flumazenil 0.2 mg IV (use cautiously; may precipitate seizures in chronic benzodiazepine users).

Applied Case Scenario

Scene: You attend a 28-year-old male with a traumatic femur fracture. He is in severe pain (10/10) despite splinting. BP 110/70, HR 105, SpO₂ 97% on room air. You decide to administer fentanyl 1.5 mcg/kg IV. Patient weight 82 kg. You have 100 mcg (2 mL) ampoules of fentanyl.

Questions for discussion:

  1. Calculate the required dose in mcg.
  2. How many mL do you draw up from the 100 mcg/2 mL ampoule?
  3. Over what time period should you administer this dose, and what adverse effects will you monitor for?

Answers:

  1. Dose = 1.5 mcg/kg × 82 kg = 123 mcg.
  2. Concentration = 100 mcg ÷ 2 mL = 50 mcg/mL. Volume = 123 mcg ÷ 50 mcg/mL = 2.46 mL (draw up 2.5 mL).
  3. Administer over 1–2 minutes (slow IV push). Monitor for respiratory depression (RR <10, SpO₂ drop), hypotension, chest wall rigidity. Prepare naloxone and bag-valve-mask.

5.6 Paediatric Infusion Calculations

Paediatric drug dosing is always weight-based. A small calculation error in a neonate or infant can result in a 10-fold overdose. Golden rules:

  • Always verify weight in kilograms (if given in grams for neonates, convert: 1 kg = 1000 g).
  • Use a paediatric resuscitation tape (e.g. Broselow tape) or smartphone app to cross-check doses.
  • Use dilute concentrations where possible (e.g. dilute 1:1000 adrenaline to 1:10,000 for neonatal resuscitation).
  • Double-check all calculations with a colleague before administration.

Example 6 (paediatric): A 6-month-old infant (7 kg) in status epilepticus requires midazolam 0.15 mg/kg IV. You have 5 mg/mL ampoules.

  1. Dose = 0.15 mg/kg × 7 kg = 1.05 mg (round to 1 mg for practical administration).
  2. Volume = 1 mg ÷ 5 mg/mL = 0.2 mL.
  3. Administer slowly over 2–3 minutes; prepare airway equipment (BVM, suction, O₂).

5.7 High-Alert Medications & Error-Prone Situations

The Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) designates certain drugs as high-alert medications — those that carry a heightened risk of causing significant patient harm when used in error. In the prehospital/emergency setting, these include:

  • Opioids (morphine, fentanyl) — risk of respiratory arrest.
  • Sedatives/hypnotics (midazolam, propofol) — risk of apnoea, aspiration.
  • Vasopressors/inotropes (adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine) — risk of severe hypertension, arrhythmias, tissue necrosis (extravasation).
  • Insulin — risk of severe hypoglycaemia.
  • Anticoagulants (heparin) — risk of major haemorrhage.
  • Neuromuscular blockers (suxamethonium, rocuronium) — risk of awareness under paralysis if inadequate sedation, cannot ventilate/cannot intubate.

5.7.1 Common Calculation Errors & How to Prevent Them

Error TypeExamplePrevention Strategy
Decimal point error 0.5 mg misread as 5 mg (10-fold overdose) Write a leading zero (0.5, never .5). Avoid trailing zeros (5 mg, never 5.0 mg). Use bold or underline for decimals in written orders.
Dose vs. volume confusion Administer 1 mL instead of 1 mg (depends on concentration) Always state both dose (mg) and volume (mL) when verbalising orders. Check ampoule label for concentration before drawing up.
Incorrect weight Using lbs instead of kg, or pediatric weight in grams instead of kg Confirm weight unit. Convert before calculation. Use Broselow tape for paediatric patients.
Confusing ratio strengths Using 1:1000 adrenaline IV instead of 1:10,000 (10-fold overdose) Memorise standard concentrations for high-alert drugs. Always dilute if uncertain. Check packaging colour-coding.
Wrong rate unit Programming 50 mcg/min instead of 50 mL/hour Clarify units before programming pump. Use standardised infusion charts/protocols. Independent double-check for high-alert drugs.

5.8 Safe Infusion Practices: The Six Rights & Beyond

The Six Rights of Medication Administration form the foundation of safe practice:

  1. Right Patient — Verify identity with two identifiers (name, date of birth, ID number, wristband). Ask patient to state their name.
  2. Right Drug — Check drug name, confirm indication. Look-alike/sound-alike drugs (e.g. dopamine vs. dobutamine) require extra vigilance.
  3. Right Dose — Calculate independently. Double-check high-alert drugs with a colleague. Use a calculator; do not rely on mental arithmetic for complex calculations.
  4. Right Route — IV, IM, SC, IO (intraosseous), PO, SL, IN, PR — each has different absorption/onset. Verify the prescribed route matches the formulation (some drugs cause tissue necrosis if given IM/SC instead of IV).
  5. Right Time — Confirm timing (STAT, scheduled, PRN). Some drugs have time-critical windows (e.g. thrombolytics within 4.5 hours of stroke onset).
  6. Right Documentation — Record drug name, dose, route, time, patient response, adverse effects, and your signature/registration number in the patient care record contemporaneously.

5.8.1 Additional Safety Checks for Infusions

  • Inspect the solution: Discard if discoloured, cloudy, or contains particulate matter. Check expiry date.
  • Use the correct giving set/pump: Macrodrip vs. microdrip; dedicated line for vasopressors; PVC-free tubing for lipid-soluble drugs (propofol, diazepam).
  • Label infusions clearly: Drug name, concentration, date/time prepared, your initials. Colour-coded labels reduce confusion.
  • Secure IV access: Extravasation of vasopressors causes tissue necrosis; secure cannula with transparent dressing, check site hourly.
  • Monitor continuously: Vital signs (BP, HR, RR, SpO₂), conscious level, pain score, fluid balance (input/output chart). Use infusion pump alarms (occlusion, air-in-line, low battery).
  • Avoid multi-drug infusions through a single lumen unless drugs are known to be compatible (many drugs precipitate or inactivate each other; consult compatibility charts).
Never administer an infusion if you are uncertain about the calculation, concentration, or compatibility. Clarify with a senior paramedic, pharmacist, or medical control. It is always safer to delay and confirm than to proceed with an incorrect dose.

5.9 Infusion Pumps: Types, Programming & Troubleshooting

5.9.1 Types of Infusion Pumps

  • Volumetric pumps: Deliver set mL/hour; most common in ambulances and ICU.
  • Syringe pumps: Small volume, high precision (0.1 mL/hour increments); used for neonates, high-concentration drugs.
  • Patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pumps: Allow patient to self-administer bolus doses within programmed limits (lockout interval).
  • Enteral feeding pumps: For nasogastric/gastrostomy nutrition (not for IV use).

5.9.2 Programming an Infusion Pump: Step-by-Step

  1. Prime the infusion line (run fluid through tubing to expel all air).
  2. Load the giving set into the pump mechanism (follow manufacturer instructions; peristaltic pumps require tubing to sit in the pump cassette correctly).
  3. Enter the infusion rate in mL/hour (or mL/min for some older models).
  4. Set volume-to-be-infused (VTBI) or leave on free-flow if continuous infusion.
  5. Set alarm limits (pressure, air-in-line, low battery).
  6. Press START. Verify fluid is flowing by observing drip chamber and checking pump display.
  7. Document pump settings in patient care record.

5.9.3 Common Pump Alarms & Troubleshooting

5.10 Electrolyte & Glucose Infusions

5.10.1 Potassium Chloride (KCl) Infusion

Indication: Hypokalaemia (K⁺ <3.5 mmol/L).
Standard preparation: 40 mmol KCl in 1 L normal saline or 5% dextrose.
Maximum safe rate: 10 mmol/hour via peripheral IV; 20 mmol/hour via central line (faster rates cause cardiac arrest).
Never give IV push. KCl is a high-alert drug; accidental bolus is fatal.

5.10.2 Magnesium Sulfate Infusion

Indication: Eclampsia, pre-eclampsia, hypomagnesaemia, torsades de pointes VT.
Eclampsia dose: 4 g IV over 20 minutes (loading dose), then 1 g/hour maintenance.
Standard preparation: 20 g magnesium sulfate in 500 mL normal saline (40 mg/mL).

Calculation example: Load 4 g over 20 minutes. Concentration 40 mg/mL.

Volume = 4000 mg ÷ 40 mg/mL = 100 mL over 20 minutes = 300 mL/hour for 20 min

Toxicity signs: Loss of deep tendon reflexes, respiratory depression (keep calcium gluconate 10% at bedside as antidote).

5.10.3 Dextrose (Glucose) Infusions

Indication: Hypoglycaemia, maintenance fluid (with saline), TPN component.
Concentrations available:

  • 5% dextrose (D5W): 5 g glucose per 100 mL = 50 g/L. Isotonic in bag, becomes hypotonic once glucose is metabolised. Used for maintenance fluid, drug dilution.
  • 10% dextrose (D10W): 100 g/L. Used for moderate hypoglycaemia (neonates, unable to take oral).
  • 50% dextrose (D50W): 500 g/L = 0.5 g/mL. Hypertonic; used for severe hypoglycaemia in adults (25 g IV bolus = 50 mL of 50% dextrose).

Hypoglycaemia treatment (adult): 25 g IV dextrose. If using 50% dextrose: 50 mL IV push. If using 10% dextrose: 250 mL IV over 10 minutes.

5.11 Blood Product Transfusion

Blood transfusion requires strict adherence to safety protocols to prevent ABO incompatibility (immediate haemolytic transfusion reaction, potentially fatal).

5.11.1 Pre-Transfusion Checks

  1. Two-person verification: Check patient identity (wristband) and blood unit label (patient name, hospital number, blood group, unit number, expiry date) with a second qualified person.
  2. ABO/Rh compatibility: Verify patient blood group matches unit label. Cross-match sticker must be present.
  3. Inspect blood bag: Check for discolouration, clots, leaks. Blood should be refrigerator-cold (stored at 2–6°C).
  4. Baseline observations: Record BP, HR, RR, temp before starting transfusion.

5.11.2 Transfusion Procedure

  • Use a blood administration set with 170-micron filter (traps clots/debris).
  • Prime set with 0.9% saline (never use dextrose; causes haemolysis).
  • Start transfusion slowly (50 mL over first 15 minutes) while observing patient for acute reactions.
  • If no reaction, increase to prescribed rate (1 unit PRBC over 2–4 hours; faster in massive haemorrhage).
  • Monitor every 15 minutes for first hour, then hourly: BP, HR, RR, temp. Stop immediately if fever, rash, chest pain, dyspnoea, or hypotension develops.

5.11.3 Transfusion Reactions

Reaction TypeOnsetSigns/SymptomsManagement
Acute haemolytic Immediate (ABO incompatibility) Fever, rigors, chest/back pain, hypotension, haemoglobinuria (red urine), DIC STOP transfusion immediately. IV fluids, support BP, contact blood bank, return unit for analysis.
Febrile non-haemolytic During or within 4 hours Fever ≥1°C rise, chills (no haemolysis) Slow/stop transfusion, paracetamol, monitor. If isolated fever with no other signs, may resume slowly after 30 min.
Allergic/anaphylaxis Minutes to hours Urticaria (mild), or angioedema, bronchospasm, hypotension (severe) STOP transfusion. Mild: antihistamine (chlorphenamine 10 mg IV). Severe: adrenaline 0.5 mg IM, IV fluids, salbutamol.
TRALI (Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury) Within 6 hours Sudden dyspnoea, hypoxia, bilateral pulmonary infiltrates (non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema) STOP transfusion. O₂, consider intubation/ventilation, supportive care. Do not give diuretics (worsens outcome).
TACO (Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload) During or within 6 hours Dyspnoea, hypertension, JVP elevation, pulmonary oedema (cardiogenic) STOP/slow transfusion. Sit patient upright, O₂, diuretic (furosemide 40 mg IV).

5.12 Documentation & Medico-Legal Considerations

Every infusion must be documented in the patient care record with the following details (see Module 1 for full record-keeping standards):

  • Date and time infusion commenced.
  • Drug name (generic and trade name if relevant), dose, concentration, total volume, diluent used.
  • Route (IV peripheral/central, IO, SC) and cannula/line site (e.g. left antecubital fossa, 18G cannula).
  • Infusion rate (mL/hour or gtt/min) and pump settings (if used).
  • Indication for infusion (e.g. "severe pain post femur fracture", "hypotension unresponsive to fluid bolus").
  • Patient response: vital signs, pain score, conscious level at start and every 15–30 minutes during infusion.
  • Adverse effects (if any) and interventions taken.
  • Time infusion completed or stopped, total volume infused.
  • Signature, name, registration number.
Medico-legal principle: "If it is not documented, it did not happen." In the event of an adverse outcome, your patient care record is the primary legal document examined by expert witnesses, the HPCSA, and courts. A missing or illegible drug dose, or an uncorrected calculation error in your notes, is indefensible (see PricewaterhouseCoopers Inc v National Potato Co-operative Ltd 2004 (6) SA 66 (SCA), Module 1, on the evidentiary weight of contemporaneous records).

5.14 Practical Calculation Exercises

Test your understanding with these practice scenarios. Answers are provided below each question.

Exercise 1: Morphine Bolus

Administer 7 mg morphine IV. Available: 10 mg/mL ampoule. How many mL do you draw up?

Show Answer

Volume = 7 mg ÷ 10 mg/mL = 0.7 mL

Exercise 2: Adrenaline for Anaphylaxis

Patient in anaphylaxis, prescribed 0.5 mg adrenaline IM. You have 1:1000 (1 mg/mL) ampoules. How many mL do you administer?

Show Answer

Volume = 0.5 mg ÷ 1 mg/mL = 0.5 mL IM into anterolateral thigh

Exercise 3: Noradrenaline Infusion

Prescribed: Noradrenaline 0.15 mcg/kg/min. Patient weight: 58 kg. Preparation: 8 mg noradrenaline in 500 mL 5% dextrose. Calculate infusion rate in mL/hour.

Show Answer

1. Dose per minute: 0.15 mcg/kg/min × 58 kg = 8.7 mcg/min
2. Dose per hour: 8.7 × 60 = 522 mcg/hour = 0.522 mg/hour
3. Concentration: 8 mg in 500 mL = 0.016 mg/mL
4. Infusion rate: 0.522 mg/hour ÷ 0.016 mg/mL = 32.625 mL/hour (round to 32.6 or 33 mL/hour)

Exercise 4: Gravity Drip Rate

Infuse 1500 mL Hartmann's over 12 hours using a 15 gtt/mL macrodrip set. Calculate drop rate in gtt/min.

Show Answer

1. Time in minutes: 12 hours × 60 = 720 min
2. Drop rate: (1500 mL × 15 gtt/mL) ÷ 720 min = 22,500 ÷ 720 = 31.25 gtt/min (round to 31 gtt/min)

Exercise 5: Paediatric Ketamine

3-year-old child (15 kg) requires procedural sedation for fracture reduction. Prescribed: Ketamine 1.5 mg/kg IV. Available: 50 mg/mL vial. Calculate dose and volume.

Show Answer

1. Dose: 1.5 mg/kg × 15 kg = 22.5 mg
2. Volume: 22.5 mg ÷ 50 mg/mL = 0.45 mL
