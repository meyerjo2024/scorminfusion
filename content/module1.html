Infusion Therapy & Drug Calculations

1. Introduction to Infusion Therapy in Prehospital & Critical Care

Intravenous infusion therapy is a critical intervention in paramedic practice, enabling precise delivery of medications, fluids, blood products, and electrolytes directly into the circulatory system. Mastery of infusion calculations is essential for patient safety — calculation errors can result in under-dosing (therapeutic failure), overdosing (toxicity, cardiac arrest, respiratory depression), or incorrect fluid administration rates (fluid overload, pulmonary oedema).

2. Clinical Approach: CCT & CDST Framework

2.1 Critical Care Transfer (CCT) Approach

When managing infusion therapy in the prehospital and inter-facility transfer context, apply the CCT framework:

C
Continuous Monitoring
Vital signs, ECG, SpO₂, ETCO₂, and level of consciousness must be monitored continuously. Document baseline observations before, during, and after infusion.
C
Catheter & Line Assessment
Verify IV cannula patency, site integrity, and infusion device function. Check for signs of infiltration, phlebitis, or extravasation.
T
Transport Preparation
Ensure adequate supply of infusion fluid, drugs, and backup equipment. Calculate total infusion duration with 30 minutes contingency.

2.2 Clinical Decision Support Tool (CDST)

Use the CDST framework for every infusion decision:

1
Indication — What is the clinical indication for this infusion?
Verify against protocols/guidelines
2
Drug — Is this the correct drug, dose, and route?
Double-check calculation, concentration, compatibilities
3
Patient — Does the patient have allergies or comorbidities?
Adjust for weight, renal/hepatic impairment
4
Equipment — Is the correct administration set available?
Confirm pump compatibility, drip set type
5
Monitoring — What observations are required?
Set parameters, document response
6
Contingency — What are the risks and backup plan?
Have reversal agents, alternative access ready

3. Types of Intravenous Infusions

Understanding the type of infusion prescribed determines the calculation method, equipment required, and monitoring frequency.

3.1 Continuous IV Infusion

Medications or fluids administered at a constant, uninterrupted rate over an extended period. Common examples include maintenance fluids, vasopressors, sedatives, and analgesics.

3.2 Intermittent IV Infusion

Medications given at discrete intervals, typically over 20–60 minutes, via a secondary IV line. Examples: antibiotics, antiemetics, some analgesics.

3.3 IV Bolus

Rapid administration of a medication directly into the vein. Used for emergency medications where immediate effect is required.

3.4 Blood Product Transfusion

Administration of blood or blood products requiring specific protocols, consent, and monitoring for transfusion reactions.

4. Types of Infusion Pumps

4.1 Volumetric Infusion Pumps

Large-volume pumps designed for continuous infusions. They deliver fluids at a programmed rate (mL/hour) and are ideal for maintenance fluids, blood products, and continuous medication infusions.

4.2 Syringe Drivers / Syringe Pumps

Compact pumps that push the plunger of a syringe to deliver precise, low-volume infusions. Essential for critical care medications requiring exact dosing (e.g., vasopressors, sedatives, analgesics).

4.3 Target-Controlled Infusion (TCI) Pumps

Advanced computerized pumps that calculate and deliver drug doses based on pharmacokinetic models to achieve a target plasma or effect-site concentration. Used primarily for anaesthetic agents (propofol, remifentanil).

TCI Pump Features

  • Modes: Plasma concentration target vs. Effect-site concentration target
  • Built-in pharmacokinetic models: Marsh, Schnider, Minto, Eleveld
  • Real-time display: Current calculated concentration, predicted effect
  • Safety features: Maximum dose limits, syringe recognition, drug library

Paramedic relevance: While primarily used in theatre and ICU, understanding TCI principles is important for inter-facility transfers of sedated patients.

4.4 Patient-Controlled Analgesia (PCA) Pumps

Allow patients to self-administer preset doses of analgesic medication within safety limits. Include lockout periods to prevent overdose.

4.5 Elastomeric Pumps

Disposable, non-electronic pumps using elastic membrane pressure to deliver medication. Useful for ambulatory infusions and home care.

5. Drug Concentration & Dilution Principles

Drug concentrations may be expressed as:

  • mg/mL: milligrams per millilitre (most common)
  • mcg/mL: micrograms per millilitre (for potent drugs)
  • % w/v: percentage weight/volume (e.g., 0.9% NaCl = 0.9 g/100 mL)
  • Ratio strength: 1:1000, 1:10,000 (parts of drug per parts of solution)
  • Units/mL: for biologicals (insulin, heparin)

6. Infusion Rate Calculations

6.1 Basic Formula

Volume (mL) ÷ Time (hours) = Rate (mL/hour)

Example:

Order: 1000 mL normal saline over 8 hours.

Rate = 1000 mL ÷ 8 hours = 125 mL/hour

6.2 Gravity Drip Calculations

Drop rate (gtt/min) = (Volume × Drop factor) ÷ Time (minutes)

IV Drip Flow Rates and Drop Factor

6.3 Weight-Based Dosing

Dose (mcg/kg/min) × Weight (kg) × 60 ÷ Concentration (mcg/mL) = Rate (mL/hour)

8. Paediatric Infusion Calculations

Paediatric dosing requires additional safety measures:

  • Always calculate based on actual weight (mg/kg, mcg/kg/min)
  • Use Broselow tape for emergency weight estimation
  • Prefer microdrip sets (60 gtt/mL) for accurate low-volume infusions
  • Double-check all calculations with a second practitioner

9. High-Alert Medications

Medications with high risk of causing significant patient harm when used in error:

  • Insulin
  • Opioids (morphine, fentanyl)
  • Vasopressors (adrenaline, noradrenaline)
  • Potassium chloride concentrate
  • Heparin
  • Neuromuscular blocking agents

Safety Strategies

  • Independent double-check of drug, dose, and rate
  • Use standard concentrations where possible
  • Label all infusion lines clearly
  • Never ignore pump alarms

12. Blood Product Transfusion

Key principles for safe blood product administration:

  • Verify patient identity and blood product using two identifiers
  • Check blood group compatibility (ABO, Rh)
  • Monitor vital signs: baseline, 15 min, then hourly
  • Complete transfusion within 4 hours of removal from cold storage

Transfusion Reaction Signs

Fever, chills, rash, dyspnoea, hypotension, back pain, dark urine

Immediate action: Stop transfusion, maintain IV access with normal saline, notify medical team, send blood sample for haemolysis screen.

13. Documentation & Medico-Legal Considerations

Comprehensive documentation must include:

  • Drug name, concentration, dose, and route
  • Calculation method and verification
  • Time started and completed
  • Patient response and any adverse events
  • Pump settings and any rate changes

Legal Principle

"If it is not documented, it did not happen." Your patient care record is the primary legal document examined in adverse outcomes.
